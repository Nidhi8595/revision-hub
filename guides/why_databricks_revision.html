🧱 Why Databricks — the architecture argument, not the feature list
Databricks isn't interesting because of any one feature — it's interesting because of how storage, compute, engineering, governance, and workloads all sit around ONE shared data foundation. This sheet follows that same thread: tables wherever something is genuinely comparable, flip cards wherever a strength has a real catch attached to it, and a proper flashcard deck at the end for the facts you just need to know cold.

The trade-off that runs through this entire document: wiring more of these layers together through one platform cuts down the number of integration points you have to design and babysit — but it also quietly increases how much your architecture leans on Databricks-specific services and patterns. Neither side is free. That tension shows up again and again below.

Delta Lake, quickly IT'S NOT THE STORAGE

What it isA transaction log sitting on top of Parquet files
What it addsACID transactions, scalable metadata handling, a reliable "table" behavior
What it is notThe physical storage itself — that's still just cloud object storage underneath
Nice side effectSame table layer supports both batch AND streaming, so you're not maintaining two versions of "the same" data

Open data ≠ open platform THE QUESTION EVERYONE FORGETS TO ASK

An open table format makes the data easier to reach from other engines. It says nothing about whether your governance rules, pipeline definitions, or platform-specific tuning come along for the ride. Portability has to be checked layer by layer — not just by glancing at the file format and calling it a day.
Worth remembering"We use an open format" and "we're not locked in" are two different sentences. Only one of them is usually true.

The benchmark claim worth knowing: Photon is cited at up to 5x better price/performance vs other cloud data warehouses on TPC-DS benchmarks. Treat that as a vendor number, not gospel — but it tells you where Databricks thinks the engine earns its keep.
The actual point being made here: this isn't "Spark vs SQL." Databricks runs SQL workloads just fine while still keeping the broader distributed-processing model underneath for engineering, streaming, and everything else that isn't a tidy SQL query.
Layer 3

Lakeflow — Ingestion, Transformation, Orchestration

Three jobs that usually live in three different tools, bundled into one instead.
PieceJob
Lakeflow ConnectGets data into the platform (ingestion)
Lakeflow PipelinesTransforms it — declaratively, meaning you describe the end state, not every execution step
Lakeflow JobsKeeps the whole thing scheduled and running (orchestration)
Important boundary: Lakeflow sits above storage and compute, it doesn't replace them. The actual processing still runs on Databricks' execution infrastructure (Spark/Photon) underneath. It's also not trying to replace every external orchestration tool on earth — it's the Databricks-native way of building and running these workflows, nothing more dramatic than that.

Why bundle these three together at all? THE REAL ANSWER

Not because ingestion and orchestration are hard problems nobody's solved — they're not, there are dozens of tools for each. The actual benefit is fewer boundaries between ingestion, transformation, orchestration, compute, and governance. Every boundary is a place where something can silently break, need its own monitoring, or just add latency while data waits to cross it.
Layer 4

Unity Catalog — Governance That Follows the Data

Not a processing layer. Not a product bolted on the side. It's the thing watching everything else.
What it tracksIn plain terms
Who can access itPrivileges on catalogs, schemas, tables, and other securable objects
What they can do with itRow filters, column masks, attribute-based policies for finer-grained control
Where the data came fromLineage — automatically captured, including column-level for supported workloads
What depends on itDownstream lineage through tables, queries, jobs, notebooks, pipelines
What activity occurredAuditing
The boundary worth remembering — this one gets glossed over a lot: Unity Catalog governs registered assets and supported Databricks governance paths. It does not automatically mean every external system in your org, or every scrap of infrastructure outside Databricks, suddenly answers to it. External systems keep their own permissions. Governance doesn't magically reach outside the platform just because it's thorough inside it.
One-linerGovernance connected to the actual data flow beats governance as a separate checklist nobody checks.

The point isn't that SQL, streaming, ML, and AI secretly become the same workload — they don't, and never will. The point is they can all operate around the same governed data and metadata, instead of each one needing a private copy nobody else trusts.
The "not everything has to move" option

Right choice depends on the workload, who owns the data, performance requirements, and plain operational boundaries. There's no universally correct answer here — anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.
